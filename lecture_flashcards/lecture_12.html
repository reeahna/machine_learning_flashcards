Why Dimensionality Reduction?
▼
  • High-dimensional data has the curse of dimensionality: instances are sparse, distances become less meaningful, and models train slowly.
  • Two main approaches: Projection (project onto a lower-dimensional subspace) and Manifold Learning (unroll a manifold).
  • Important: reducing dimensions loses some information. It is a form of lossy compression.
  • Uses: speed up training, remove noise, aid visualization.
🔢
PCA: Principal Component Analysis
▼
  • PCA is the most popular dimensionality reduction algorithm. Identifies the hyperplane closest to the data and projects onto it.
  • First principal component (PC1): the axis that accounts for the largest amount of variance.
  • PC2: orthogonal to PC1, accounts for the next largest remaining variance.
  • All principal components are orthogonal to each other.
  • PCA automatically centers the data. Sensitive to feature scaling — scale features before applying PCA.
  • Explained variance ratio: available via explained_variance_ratio_. Tells you what fraction of total variance each PC captures.
Explained variance ratio
explained_variance_ratio_[i] = variance of PC_i / total variance
📊
PCA in Practice
▼
  • Choose number of components by setting a threshold for cumulative explained variance (e.g., 95%).
  • Example: MNIST 784 features → 154 features preserving 95% of variance.
  • PCA can be used for compression — project to lower dimensions, then inverse_transform to reconstruct.
  • Reconstruction error: the mean squared distance between original and reconstructed data.
  • Scikit-Learn: PCA(n_components=0.95) automatically finds the minimum number of dimensions to preserve 95% of variance.
🌀
Manifold Learning & LLE
▼
  • Manifold: a d-dimensional shape embedded in higher-dimensional space. The Swiss roll is a 2D manifold in 3D space.
  • Manifold hypothesis: most real-world high-dimensional data lies near a much lower-dimensional manifold.
  • LLE (Locally Linear Embedding): a nonlinear dimensionality reduction technique that does not rely on projections.
  • LLE step 1: for each instance, find how it can be reconstructed as a linear combination of its k nearest neighbors.
  • LLE step 2: find a low-dimensional representation that best preserves these local linear relationships.
  • LLE computational complexity: O(m²) in the last step — scales poorly to very large datasets.
LLE complexity
O(m log(m)n log(k)) + O(mnk³) + O(dm²)
Last O(dm²) term makes LLE slow on large datasets
